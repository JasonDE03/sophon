Episode 8 · 1927

The uncertainty principle: you cannot know it all at once

An electron leaves a clear track in a cloud chamber, like a particle following a path. But quantum mechanics has no place for paths. Early in 1927, a 25-year-old physicist in Copenhagen resolved this contradiction with an inequality.

Summary

In 1927, Werner Heisenberg showed that a particle’s position and momentum cannot both be known to arbitrary precision: the more precisely one is known, the less certain the other becomes. The underlying reason is that an electron is also a wave, and a wave confined to a small region must be made up of many different wavelengths. This limit comes from the nature of things themselves, rather than from poor measuring instruments. For objects the size of a dust grain or larger, it is too small ever to notice.

The track in the cloud chamber

By the end of 1926, quantum mechanics had two mathematical forms, Heisenberg’s matrix mechanics and Schrödinger’s wave mechanics from episode 7, and Born had proposed that the wave gave probabilities. But the question “what does the electron actually do?” remained open. Matrix mechanics had no paths. Yet in a cloud chamber, where tiny water droplets condense along a charged particle’s flight, an electron left a clear track like the path of a bullet.

Heisenberg was then a lecturer at Bohr’s institute in Copenhagen. The two argued about this all winter, so intensely that both became exhausted. In February 1927, Bohr went skiing in Norway for about a month, leaving Heisenberg on his own.

According to Heisenberg’s memoirs, the idea came to him during a night-time walk in the park behind the institute: perhaps the cloud-chamber track was not a precise path. It was only a chain of water droplets, each far larger than the electron, giving only an approximate position. The right question was not “where is the electron?” but “how precisely can we know its position and velocity?”

Do the experiment yourself

The simulation has two parts. The first lets you build a wave packet from waves of different wavelengths, to see why position and momentum cannot both be sharply defined. The second recreates the thought experiment Heisenberg himself used in 1927: looking at an electron through a microscope.

To see an electron’s position more clearly, we illuminate it with light of a shorter wavelength. What happens to the electron?

Shorter-wavelength light gives a sharper image, but each of its light particles carries more momentum $h/\lambda$, so it kicks the electron harder. The sharper the image, the harder it is to know the electron’s velocity afterwards. Try “The gamma-ray microscope”.

Notice:

  • In “Build a wave packet”, mixing only waves with similar wavelengths gives a narrow momentum spectrum but a wide packet: the electron is “somewhere” across several nanometres.
  • Increase the momentum spread: the wave packet contracts. The two always trade off, and the product $\Delta x\cdot\Delta p$ stays at $\hbar/2$. There is no way to make both small.
  • In “The gamma-ray microscope”, move the wavelength from visible light down to gamma rays: the image spot shrinks, but the kick arrow grows longer. In the bars below, as Δx gets shorter, Δp gets correspondingly longer, while the product bar $\Delta x\cdot\Delta p$ stays at $h$.
  • Widen the lens to collect more light: the image becomes sharper, but we do not know which way the light particle scattered, so the kick becomes more uncertain. The product remains of order $h$.

The fourteen-page letter

On 23 February 1927, Heisenberg wrote Wolfgang Pauli a fourteen-page letter setting out the idea. Pauli was the fiercest critic Heisenberg knew, and his response was very positive. Heisenberg wrote up a paper, received by Zeitschrift für Physik on 23 March 1927.

To explain it, Heisenberg used a thought experiment: to find out where an electron is, look at it through a microscope. The microscope’s resolution is limited by the wavelength of light, so a precise position requires a very short wavelength, meaning gamma rays. But a short-wavelength light particle carries a large momentum and strikes the electron like a ball in the Compton effect, changing its velocity by an unpredictable amount. He concluded that the product of the two uncertainties was of order Planck’s constant, $\Delta x\cdot\Delta p \sim h$, and wrote a famous sentence: “The 'path' comes into existence only when we observe it.”

Bohr returns

Bohr returned in mid-March and read the manuscript. He agreed with the conclusion but identified an error in the microscope analysis: Heisenberg had neglected the lens aperture. According to Bohr, the uncertainty came not just from the collision, but from the fact that both light and electrons were waves as well as particles. The two argued fiercely for weeks.

In the end, Heisenberg kept the paper as it was but added a “note added in proof” acknowledging Bohr’s comments. That same year, the American physicist Earle Kennard rigorously proved that, for every state, the spreads in position and momentum satisfy $\Delta x\cdot\Delta p \geq \hbar/2$, exactly the form found in textbooks today. In September 1927, Bohr presented his own interpretation, called the complementarity principle, at a conference in Como, Italy.

Just enough maths (optional)

Let $\Delta x$ be the spread in position and $\Delta p$ the spread in momentum, measured as standard deviations. Kennard’s inequality is

$$\Delta x\,\Delta p \;\geq\; \frac{\hbar}{2},\qquad \hbar = \frac{h}{2\pi} \approx 1.055\times10^{-34}\ \text{J}\cdot\text{s}.$$

It follows from a general property of all waves: a packet of width $\Delta x$ must contain wave numbers $k = 2\pi/\lambda$ spread over a range $\Delta k \geq 1/(2\Delta x)$. According to de Broglie, $p = \hbar k$, so multiplying both sides by $\hbar$ gives the inequality above. A bell-shaped (Gaussian) packet achieves exact equality.

For an electron in an atom, $\Delta x \approx 0.1$ nm, so $\Delta p \geq 5\times10^{-25}$ kg·m/s, giving a velocity uncertainty of about $6\times10^5$ m/s, comparable to the electron’s own velocity in the atom. An electron cannot have a path in the classical sense. For a dust grain of mass 1 microgram located to within 1 micrometre, the velocity uncertainty is only about $5\times10^{-20}$ m/s, too small ever to measure.

More: calculating Heisenberg’s microscope

A microscope with aperture half-angle $\varepsilon$ can distinguish two points separated by $\Delta x \approx \lambda/(2\sin\varepsilon)$. The light particle has momentum $h/\lambda$; after scattering from the electron, it enters the lens in any direction within the aperture, so the transverse momentum transferred to the electron is uncertain over a range $\Delta p \approx 2(h/\lambda)\sin\varepsilon$. Multiplying gives $\Delta x\,\Delta p \approx h$, independent of wavelength or aperture angle. This is the version corrected following Bohr’s comments, which Heisenberg presented in his 1930 Chicago lectures; the 1927 paper neglected the aperture angle.

Common misconceptions

“Uncertainty comes from measuring instruments that are not good enough.” No instrument can beat this limit, because it is part of the electron’s own wave nature. An electron does not have both a sharp position and a sharp momentum waiting to be measured.

“Uncertainty is just the disturbance caused by measurement.” That was Heisenberg’s explanation using the microscope in 1927, and it is easy to picture. But Kennard’s inequality concerns the spread of the state itself, without referring to any measurement. As early as 1927, Bohr thought the explanation in terms of a kick had not reached the root of the matter.

“Heisenberg proved $\Delta x\cdot\Delta p \geq \hbar/2$.” Heisenberg’s 1927 paper gave only an order-of-magnitude estimate, $\Delta x\cdot\Delta p \sim h$. The exact inequality was Kennard’s, from the same year, and the general form for any pair of quantities was Howard Robertson’s, from 1929.

Quiz

What must we do to confine a wave packet more tightly in space?

A wave with a single wavelength extends throughout space. To confine it, we must mix many wavelengths, and therefore many different momenta. The tighter the position, the wider the momentum spread.

Why do we not notice the uncertainty principle with a ball or a dust grain?

The limit concerns momentum, which is mass multiplied by velocity. For a heavy object, the velocity uncertainty is unimaginably small.

Who identified the error in Heisenberg’s original microscope analysis?

Bohr pointed out that Heisenberg had neglected the lens aperture, and that uncertainty was rooted in wave–particle duality, rather than just the collision. Heisenberg added a note to his paper.

What came next

The uncertainty principle became a pillar of the interpretation of quantum mechanics developed by Bohr and Heisenberg in Copenhagen. It explains why atoms do not collapse: if an electron fell into the nucleus, its position would become too sharply defined, and its momentum, and therefore its energy, would shoot up.

But the role of waves in the theory still rested on arguments. Did electrons really interfere like light? That same year, 1927, an experiment in New York that began with a laboratory accident answered the question.
