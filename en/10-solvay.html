Episode 10 · 1927

The Solvay conference: Bohr and Einstein

In October 1927, almost everyone who had built quantum physics sat together in a lecture hall in Brussels. They posed for a famous photograph, then began a debate that would last nearly thirty years.

Summary

The fifth Solvay conference, in October 1927, was the first occasion on which the new quantum mechanics was discussed before the full gathering of leading physicists. Born and Heisenberg declared the theory complete. Einstein did not dispute that it was correct, but did not believe it was the whole story, and over breakfast he repeatedly proposed thought experiments to challenge Bohr. The debate did not end in Brussels: only experiments in the second half of the twentieth century could answer Einstein’s central question.

Five years of upheaval

From episode 5 to episode 9, in just five years, quantum physics had changed completely: spin, matter waves, matrix mechanics, Schrödinger’s equation, Born’s probability interpretation, the uncertainty principle and electron diffraction. Bohr’s model of 1913, with its circular orbits, had been replaced by a theory that was far more precise but much harder to picture.

In September 1927, at a conference in Como, Bohr presented his interpretation, called the complementarity principle: wave and particle are mutually exclusive aspects of the same reality, and an experiment can reveal only one. Einstein was not at Como. The two first confronted each other over this question a month later, in Brussels.

Do the experiment yourself

The Solvay debates used thought experiments: experiments nobody could perform at the time, but whose results could be deduced from the laws of physics alone. The simulation recreates two of them. The first is Einstein’s version of the double-slit experiment, as recalled by Bohr in 1949: the first slit hangs on springs, so its recoil can reveal which slit the electron passes through. The second is the objection Einstein raised publicly in the conference’s general discussion.

Einstein wanted to measure the first slit’s recoil to find out which slit the electron passed through, while still seeing interference fringes. According to Bohr, what happens?

To measure the recoil precisely enough to identify the path, we must know the slit’s momentum very precisely. By the uncertainty principle from episode 8, its position then becomes blurred, and that blur is enough to erase the fringes. We cannot both know the path and see the fringes. Brussels, October 1927

The Solvay conferences were established by the foundation of the Belgian industrialist Ernest Solvay, each inviting only a few dozen scientists. The fifth met from 24 to 29 October 1927, on the theme “Electrons and photons”, chaired by Hendrik Lorentz. Of the 29 people in the group photograph, 17 had received or would receive Nobel Prizes, including Planck, Einstein, Bohr, Marie Curie, de Broglie, Born, Heisenberg, Schrödinger, Dirac and Pauli.

The main reports followed the sequence of the preceding episodes: Compton on X-ray scattering, de Broglie on matter waves, Born and Heisenberg on matrix mechanics, and Schrödinger on wave mechanics. De Broglie presented a theory in which the electron was a real particle guided by a wave; he faced much criticism and abandoned it shortly afterwards. Born and Heisenberg ended their report by declaring quantum mechanics a closed theory.

Einstein spoke in the general discussion on 28 October. He imagined an electron passing through a small hole, its wave spreading uniformly over a hemispherical screen. If that wave described a single electron, then when the electron was detected at one point, the wave everywhere else must instantly cease to have any effect. According to Einstein, this interpretation required “an entirely peculiar mechanism of action at a distance”. He thought ψ described only an ensemble of many electrons, rather than the whole truth about a single one.

Breakfasts in Brussels

The most famous exchanges were not recorded in the conference proceedings. According to accounts given by Heisenberg and Bohr many years later, the physicists stayed at the same hotel. Each morning, Einstein proposed a new thought experiment intended to show that measurements could be more precise than the uncertainty principle allowed. Bohr, Heisenberg and Pauli analysed it all day, and by dinner Bohr had usually found the flaw.

Paul Ehrenfest, a close friend of both men, wrote to his students just after the conference: “Like a game of chess, Einstein all the time with new examples… Bohr from out of philosophical smoke clouds constantly searching for the tools to crush one example after the other. Einstein like a jack-in-the-box: jumping out fresh every morning.” Ehrenfest sided with Bohr.

A year earlier, Einstein had written to Born the words that later became famous: “Quantum mechanics is certainly imposing. But an inner voice tells me that it is not yet the real thing… I, at any rate, am convinced that He does not throw dice.” According to Heisenberg’s recollections, Bohr replied in Brussels that it cannot be for us to tell God how he is to run the world.

Just enough maths (optional)

Bohr’s argument about the recoiling slit can be calculated. Let the separation between the two slits be $d$, the distance from the first slit to the pair of slits be $L$, and the electron wavelength be $\lambda$. Electrons travelling towards the upper and lower slits give the first slit recoils differing by

$$\delta p \approx \frac{h}{\lambda}\,\frac{d}{L}.$$

To distinguish the two paths, we must measure the first slit’s momentum more precisely than $\delta p$. By the uncertainty principle, its position is then uncertain by at least about $\Delta x \approx h/\delta p = \lambda L/d$. But $\lambda L/d$ is exactly the distance between neighbouring bright fringes on the screen, if the screen is also a distance $L$ from the two slits. Moving the first slit by about one fringe spacing shifts the fringes along with it, so they overlap and disappear.

Today this can be written more compactly as a duality relation: if $D$ is path distinguishability and $V$ is fringe visibility, both ranging from 0 to 1, then

$$V^2 + D^2 \leq 1.$$
More: what really worried Einstein

Bohr, Heisenberg and Ehrenfest understood Einstein to be trying to prove the uncertainty principle wrong. More recent historians, such as Don Howard, Guido Bacciagaluppi and Antony Valentini, point out that Einstein’s public objection in the general discussion concerned something else: the “action at a distance” when a single particle’s wave extends throughout space, yet a measurement at one point instantly affects every other point. This concern later became the EPR paper of 1935, and the question that John Bell turned into something measurable in 1964.

Common misconceptions

“Einstein rejected quantum mechanics.” Einstein acknowledged that the theory correctly predicted experiments. What he did not accept was that it gave a complete description of reality. He believed there must be a deeper theory, of which quantum mechanics was only a statistical version.

“Einstein’s light box was at Solvay in 1927.” The most famous thought experiment, a box of light suspended on springs that Bohr had to refute using Einstein’s own general relativity, belonged to the sixth Solvay conference in 1930.

“At Solvay in 1927, Bohr defeated Einstein and the Copenhagen interpretation won.” The proceedings show that Bohr and Einstein spoke relatively little, and the conference actually discussed several interpretations, including de Broglie’s pilot wave. The “Bohr won” version comes mainly from the accounts of Bohr, Heisenberg and Ehrenfest. Einstein’s question about action at a distance had not been answered in Brussels.

Quiz

What was Einstein’s objection to quantum mechanics?

Einstein himself proposed light particles in 1905, and acknowledged that quantum mechanics made correct predictions. He simply did not believe it was the whole story.

According to Bohr’s argument, why can we not both know which slit the electron uses and see fringes?

The uncertainty principle applies to the slit itself. Knowing its momentum precisely enough to infer the path makes its position uncertain by about one fringe spacing, and the fringes disappear.

Where did the idea that “God does not play dice” originate?

Einstein expressed this idea in a letter to Max Born in December 1926, nearly a year before Solvay. He repeated it many times afterwards.

What came next

In 1935, Einstein, Boris Podolsky and Nathan Rosen wrote the EPR paper, arguing that quantum mechanics was incomplete because it allowed two distant particles to be connected in a way Einstein later, in 1947, called “spooky action at a distance”. Bohr replied, and neither ever convinced the other. Einstein died in 1955, Bohr in 1962.

In 1964, John Bell showed that Einstein’s question could be tested experimentally. Experiments by John Clauser in 1972, Alain Aspect in 1982 and many later groups, including Anton Zeilinger’s, showed that the world does not work as Einstein hoped: quantum correlations are real and cannot be explained by pre-existing properties of each particle. The three received the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics. Today, the very phenomenon Einstein found peculiar underlies quantum cryptography and quantum computers.
