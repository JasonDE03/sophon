Episode 9 · 1925–1927

Davisson–Germer: electrons interfere

A flask of liquid air exploded in a New York laboratory, damaging a metal sample. Two years later, that same damaged sample showed electrons interfering like waves, just as de Broglie had predicted.

Summary

At Bell Labs, Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer fired electrons at a block of nickel to study how electrons bounced off metal, with no intention of testing de Broglie’s idea. After an accident in 1925, the nickel recrystallised into a few large crystals, and the electrons bounced back much more strongly at certain angles. In 1927, they showed that these angles agreed with the electrons’ de Broglie wavelength: the crystal diffracted electrons just like X-rays. That same year, George Thomson in Scotland saw something similar by a different method, and matter waves became an experimental fact.

A question from the telephone industry

Clinton Davisson had worked since 1917 for Western Electric, which manufactured equipment for the American telephone system; its research department became Bell Telephone Laboratories in 1925. The vacuum tubes that amplified telephone signals worked by firing electron beams at metal plates, so the question of how electrons bounced off metal had practical value.

From 1921, Davisson and Charles Kunsman fired slow electrons at metal plates and counted the electrons emerging at each angle. They found unexpected peaks, which they explained by suggesting that electrons bounced differently depending on which atomic shell they reached. In Göttingen in 1925, the 21-year-old student Walter Elsasser read de Broglie’s thesis and proposed that these very peaks were signs of electron diffraction. Elsasser’s short paper attracted little attention, and Davisson took no notice of it.

Do the experiment yourself

The simulation has two parts. The first recreates the 1927 experiment: electrons are accelerated through a voltage of a few tens of volts, fired straight at a nickel crystal, and a detector scans around it to count the electrons bouncing back at each angle. The second is an experiment that became possible only much later: sending electrons one at a time through two narrow slits.

Fire electrons through two narrow slits, one electron at a time. After thousands of electrons, what pattern will the dots on the screen form?

Many alternating bright and dark stripes: the interference fringes of a wave passing through both slits, even though each electron reaches the screen as a single dot. Try “Electrons one at a time through two slits”.

Notice:

  • In “The 1927 experiment”, increase the voltage from 40 V: a “bump” rises on the polar plot, strongest at 54 V, at an angle of about 50°. This is the shape Davisson and Germer published.
  • The thin blue line is the angle calculated from the de Broglie wavelength and the 0.215 nm spacing between rows of nickel atoms. Change the voltage and the predicted angle shifts, because the wavelength changes.
  • Switch to “Particles only”: the curve is smooth, with no bump. Only waves give a peak at a particular angle.
  • In “Electrons one at a time through two slits”, the first few dozen electrons look completely random. Wait for a few thousand: fringes emerge. Close one slit, or add a detector to find out which slit the electron uses: the fringes disappear.

The exploding flask of liquid air

In April 1925, while the nickel target was being heated in a vacuum tube, a flask of liquid air exploded. The glass tube broke, air rushed in and the hot nickel became badly oxidised. To rescue the sample, Davisson and his young assistant Lester Germer heated it for a long time at high temperature to remove the oxide layer. When they measured again, the way the electrons scattered had changed completely.

It took them a while to find the reason: the heating had caused the nickel, originally made up of countless tiny crystals, to recrystallise into a few large ones. The electrons now encountered regularly arranged rows of atoms. Davisson and Germer thought the electrons were being channelled between crystal planes, but that hypothesis did not fit: the angles of the peaks changed with the electrons’ velocity.

Oxford, summer 1926

In August 1926, Davisson travelled to Britain for the meeting of the British Association for the Advancement of Science in Oxford. There he heard Max Born cite his own old curves as evidence for electron waves. Davisson showed his new data to Born and others. Schrödinger’s wave mechanics from episode 7 had just appeared, and matter waves no longer seemed far-fetched.

Back in New York, Davisson and Germer began a systematic search. Early in 1927, they found a clear peak: at a voltage of 54 V, the number of electrons bouncing back was greatest at an angle of 50°. The de Broglie wavelength of a 54 V electron agreed with the spacing between rows of nickel atoms previously measured using X-rays. Their short paper in Nature on 16 April 1927 cautiously described the results as “highly suggestive, of course, of the ideas underlying the theory of wave mechanics”.

That same year, in Aberdeen, Scotland, George Paget Thomson and his student Alexander Reid fired fast electrons through very thin metal films and saw diffraction rings on photographic film. His father, J. J. Thomson, had shown in 1897 that cathode rays consisted of charged particles: electrons. Thirty years later, the son showed that those very particles diffracted like waves. Davisson and G. P. Thomson shared the 1937 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Just enough maths (optional)

An electron accelerated through a voltage $V$ has kinetic energy $eV$, momentum $p = \sqrt{2m_e eV}$ and de Broglie wavelength

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2m_e eV}} \approx \frac{1.226}{\sqrt{V}}\ \text{nm}.$$

With $V = 54$ V, $\lambda \approx 0.167$ nm, comparable to the spacing between atoms. The electrons strike the nickel crystal face head-on, and the rows of atoms on its surface, spaced $d = 0.215$ nm apart, act as a grating. Waves reflected from neighbouring rows reinforce one another when their path difference equals one wavelength:

$$d\sin\theta = \lambda \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sin\theta = \frac{0.167}{0.215} \approx 0.776,\quad \theta \approx 50.9^\circ.$$

Davisson and Germer measured 50°. The small difference comes from the attraction of atoms as the electron enters the crystal, speeding it up slightly and shortening its wavelength inside; their full paper includes this effect.

More: why the peak is strongest at 54 V

A crystal has many layers of atoms stacked beneath its surface. The condition $d\sin\theta = \lambda$ says only that rows within the same layer reinforce one another. For a really strong peak, waves reflected from deeper layers must also be in phase, and this happens only at certain voltages. For the crystal face and direction Davisson and Germer used, the two conditions coincide near 54 V. The simulation above merely imitates the rise and fall of this bump, without calculating the full three-dimensional structure.

Common misconceptions

“Davisson and Germer performed their experiment to test de Broglie’s idea.” They had been studying electrons bouncing off metals since 1921, prompted by questions from the telephone industry. The peaks appeared thanks to an accident in 1925, and they turned to electron waves only after hearing Born in Oxford in 1926.

“Davisson and Germer performed the electron double-slit experiment.” Their experiment involved electrons reflected from a crystal. Claus Jönsson in Tübingen performed an experiment with actual double slits and electrons in 1961. Sending electrons one at a time and watching fringes gradually emerge was achieved only in 1974, by Pier Giorgio Merli, Gian Franco Missiroli and Giulio Pozzi in Bologna; in 1989, Akira Tonomura’s group at Hitachi repeated it with celebrated clarity. In The Feynman Lectures on Physics, based on lectures given in 1961–1963, Richard Feynman still described the electron double-slit experiment as one nobody had actually performed, probably because he was unaware of Jönsson’s results.

Quiz

Why was the 1925 accident important?

With a few large crystals, the electrons encountered regular rows of atoms acting as a grating, so diffraction effects appeared clearly as peaks at particular angles.

How does increasing the accelerating voltage change the diffraction angle?

The wavelength $\lambda = h/p$ decreases as the electron speeds up, and $\sin\theta = \lambda/d$ decreases with it. It was precisely this shift of angle with velocity that convinced Davisson and Germer that they were seeing diffraction.

In the double-slit experiment with individual electrons, what makes the interference fringes disappear?

Firing electrons one at a time still produces fringes. But when we know which slit the electron passed through, the fringes disappear. Episode 10 tells how Bohr and Einstein argued about this very question.

What came next

From 1927, matter waves were no longer a hypothesis. Electron diffraction became a tool for studying material surfaces, and electron microscopes emerged in the 1930s using the electrons’ very short wavelengths. Later, interference was observed with neutrons, atoms and even large molecules containing dozens of carbon atoms.

But the interference fringes of individual electrons left an uncomfortable question: which slit does an electron pass through, and why does finding out erase the fringes? In October 1927, the world’s leading physicists met in Brussels, and Einstein put that question to Bohr.
